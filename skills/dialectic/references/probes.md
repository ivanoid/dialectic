# Probe repertoire

Read this reference only when the inquiry is stalled, a deliberate elenctic test is needed, or choosing the next probe is genuinely difficult. Select one probe based on the present bottleneck. Do not expose this catalogue or run through it mechanically.

## Selection guide

- **Opening or exploring:** concrete case, unstable term, temporal change, open page.
- **Articulating or forming:** distinction, linking premise, implication, teach-back.
- **Confused or stuck:** ground condition, missing context, self-distanced replay, tentative synthesis.
- **Testing:** assumption, counterexample, steelman, cost or loss, falsifiability, behavior gap.
- **Integrating:** residue, changed question, ownership restatement, real-world return.
- **Saturated:** stop, leave unresolved, or name what can be returned to later.

## Formation probes

### Unstable term

Identify a consequential word doing more than one job. Reflect the ambiguity, then ask what the word refers to in one concrete instance. Do not replace it immediately with technical vocabulary.

### Concrete case

Ask for one episode, decision, interaction, image, or passage where the issue became real. Use it to test the abstraction rather than treating the example as mere illustration.

### Distinction

When two experiences or claims are compressed together, propose the smallest distinction that might separate them. Phrase it provisionally and let the user redraw it.

### Linking premise

When the user moves from observation to conclusion, ask what makes that move valid. Focus on the bridge, not on disputing both endpoints at once.

### Temporal change

Ask what changed between an earlier and later encounter, belief, or reaction. The human supplies lived duration; do not infer a developmental story from chronology alone.

### Teach-back

Invite the user to explain the emerging idea as if the assistant had misunderstood it. Use this when ownership is becoming blurred or the model's vocabulary has begun to dominate.

## Testing probes

### Assumption excavation

Identify the premise on which the most of the claim depends. Ask whether it is observed, inferred, inherited, or chosen, and what happens if it fails.

### Precision test

Locate a vague or universal term that protects the claim from possible failure. Ask what it includes, excludes, and what concrete evidence would distinguish it from a nearby claim.

### Strong counterexample

Offer one relevant case the claim appears unable to explain. Make it strong enough to matter but do not imply that a single exception defeats a suitably qualified claim.

### Steelman opposition

Construct the best good-faith alternative from different evidence, values, or experience. Attack the user's strongest reason rather than an incidental weakness. Use only after the user's own case is clear.

### Falsifiability or revision criterion

Ask what observation, consequence, or argument would lead the user to revise the claim. If nothing could, name that it functions as a commitment or identity rather than an empirical claim.

### Cost and loss audit

Ask what the position requires, forecloses, or makes harder: attention, relationships, identity, opportunity, reversibility, or another value. Distinguish necessary costs from costs the user merely assumes are necessary.

### Behavior gap

Compare the stated belief with repeated action or consequence. Treat inconsistency as information, not hypocrisy; behavior may reveal constraint, conflict, or a poorly specified belief.

## Dialectical probes

### Ground condition

Ask what concrete fact, resource, authority, timing, dependency, or constraint produces the apparent conflict. Do not dissolve a real value conflict into logistics, but do not romanticize logistics as existential opposition.

### Third pole

Search outside the axis organizing the apparent binary. A third pole changes the terms of the opposition; a midpoint or compromise does not.

### Residue and loss

After a synthesis or reframing, ask what it leaves out, flattens, or makes unsayable. Do not invent residue merely to keep the dialectic alive.

### Tension status

Reassess whether the tension is live, sharpened, moved, thin, or dissolved. Update the inquiry instead of preserving an earlier frame through sunk cost.

## Perspective probes

### Missing or absent

Ask what the account expected but did not contain: a person who did not respond, an emotion that did not appear, an option never considered, or a consequence omitted from the story. Let the user determine whether the absence is meaningful.

### Self-distanced replay

With consent, invite the user to recount a bounded event as a respectful observer would, preserving actions, feelings, interpretations, and unknowns. Do not use distancing to invalidate emotion or claim objectivity.

### Defamiliarization

Offer a metaphor, distant practice, or unfamiliar conceptual lens only when it can reveal a specific distinction. Translate the insight back into the original case and state where the analogy breaks.

## Return probes

### Real-world return

For a safe and reversible uncertainty, identify what future experience, behavior, response, or consequence might answer the question better than further introspection. Do not turn every contemplation into an experiment or task.

### Ownership restatement

Invite the user to state the present view, changed question, or remaining uncertainty in their own words. Do not grade the formulation; reflect what is now clearer and what remains alive.

### Deliberate incompletion

When the inquiry has reached its useful limit, name what has acquired form and what need not be resolved now. Allow a return later without manufacturing a next step.

## Probe hygiene

- Ask one substantial question at a time.
- Explain the relevance when redirecting attention.
- Do not hide advice inside a question.
- Do not make the user choose only among assistant-created categories.
- Do not repeat a probe in slightly different wording after it has failed.
- Prefer a faithful reflection over a question when the user has just disclosed something vulnerable.
- Stop when further probing produces compliance, defensiveness, repetition, or abstraction without movement.
